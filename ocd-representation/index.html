Writing a heroine with OCD

Lila plans every detail of other people's happily ever afters. She can't plan her way out of her OCD. Here's why I'm writing her, how it shows up in the book, and how I'm trying to get it right.

This page is about a novel and my approach to writing it. It isn't medical advice. If OCD is part of your life, the resources at the bottom are a good place to start.

Why I'm writing Lila

Writing a happily-ever-after rom-com for Lila, a character with OCD, is my chance to write a love story to my younger self. Growing up, I had no clue what OCD was. I never saw anyone on TV, in books, or in real life who experienced the kind of terror I felt every night when my OCD was at its worst. It felt like living in an alternate reality, where I could see hidden dangers everyone else dismissed.

Finding a therapist and getting an OCD diagnosis changed my life, because I discovered I wasn't alone. As I got older, finding friends with OCD and seeing celebrities share their experiences helped me build a community where I could learn, grow, and share my own story.

One thing I learned early on is that shame feeds the disorder. Hiding your compulsions and feeling embarrassed by the thoughts running through your head is understandable. Intrusive thoughts go directly against your values, and that's exactly why they're so scary. But keeping everything hidden only fuels the isolation.

So this book is my part in creating what I want to see in the world: an OCD story told out loud, to start conversations, help people understand, and bring OCD out of the dark. I want people with OCD to see themselves in a book. Not as a flaw, not as something to be fixed, and not as a joke. Just as part of the life they live, a life that includes rivals and romance, mistakes and successes, and most of all, happily ever afters.

Happy reading, happy wedding season, and to those of us with OCD (and everyone joining in community with us): let's go get our happily ever afters.

How I'm approaching this with care

  • Lila's OCD is never the joke. You won't find anyone saying "I'm so OCD" because they like a tidy desk.
  • A sensitivity reader will review the book before it's final. My experience is one experience of OCD, not the only one.
  • This is a rom-com with OCD representation, not a book about OCD. Lila is so much more than her OCD: she's a wedding planner, she loves making fun drinks, and she's into smart, attractive guys who challenge her.
  • The love story isn't about curing Lila's OCD. Love doesn't fix everything, and OCD isn't magically cured by finding the right guy. There's a whole life ahead for Lila after the last page, both with Nate and inside her own head.

How OCD shows up in the book

No spoilers, but here's what to expect.

  • Checking. Lila goes back to make sure, then makes sure again, because the doubt won't let go. You'll see her compulsions on the page, not just hear about them.
  • The "what if" thoughts. Intrusive worries that she's missed something, ruined something, or is about to be found out.
  • The hiding. She's very good at making it look like everything's fine at work, and it costs her.
  • The shame. Her fear that if people see the real her, they'll leave. Her best friend Henry tells her, "You think if you're not perfect, people leave. That's not how this works." She's still learning to believe him.
  • The people who stay. Nate doesn't fix her. He learns, he pays attention, and he stays.

Content warnings

Some readers like to know what's coming. The book includes:

  • On-page OCD compulsions, especially checking
  • Intrusive thoughts and anxiety, including moments of panic
  • Shame and self-criticism tied to her OCD
  • A strained parent-child relationship (Nate and his mother)

This list will be finalized before publication and printed in the book, too.

Myth vs. reality

Myth: OCD means being neat and organized.

Reality: OCD is a mental health condition built around distressing obsessions and compulsions. Some people's compulsions involve order. Many don't. (Lila's color-coded binders are her job. Her OCD is something else.)

Myth: "I'm so OCD about my desk."

Reality: OCD isn't a personality quirk. For people who live with it, it can take up hours a day and cause real suffering.

Myth: Intrusive thoughts mean you secretly want them.

Reality: The opposite. Intrusive thoughts are so upsetting because they go against what the person values.

Myth: People with OCD could just stop.

Reality: Compulsions aren't a habit you can choose to drop. OCD is treatable, and many people do very well with the right help, like ERP therapy.

Myth: The right person will fix it.

Reality: Love doesn't cure OCD. Support matters, but Lila's happy ending isn't about her OCD disappearing.

A quick glossary

Obsessions
Unwanted thoughts, images, or urges that keep coming back and cause real distress. They're not wishes, and they're not who the person is.
Compulsions
Things a person does, out loud or in their head, to quiet the anxiety an obsession causes or to prevent something they fear. The relief doesn't last, so the loop starts again.
Intrusive thoughts
Almost everyone has strange or upsetting thoughts sometimes. With OCD, those thoughts get stuck and feel urgent, dangerous, or impossible to ignore.
Checking
A common compulsion: going back to make sure, again and again, that something is locked, off, right, or safe, because the doubt won't let go. Lila's OCD shows up a lot like this.
Reassurance seeking
Asking other people, over and over, to confirm that something is okay. It helps for a minute, and then the doubt comes back.
ERP
Exposure and response prevention, a type of cognitive behavioral therapy and one of the main evidence-based treatments for OCD. Learn more from the International OCD Foundation.

Books with OCD representation

The books that shaped how I'm writing Lila, plus the romances readers with OCD recommend most, which I'm reading now.

  • Turtles All the Way Down
    Turtles All the Way Down by John Green Read it

    Aza's spiraling thoughts are some of the most honest writing about OCD I've read. It's a YA novel, not a romance, but it changed how I wanted to write Lila.

  • Beard in Mind
    Beard in Mind by Penny Reid Next on my list

    A rom-com whose heroine, Shelly, lives with OCD, and one readers with OCD recommend again and again.

  • The Charm Offensive
    The Charm Offensive by Alison Cochrun Next on my list

    A dating-show romance whose hero, Charlie, lives with OCD and anxiety.

  • Some Sort of Happy
    Some Sort of Happy by Melanie Harlow Next on my list

    A contemporary romance with a hero who has OCD.

  • OCD-UK: support for readers in the UK.
